Trust, Recovery & Evidence

Delegation Integrity

Who authorized that? Principal hierarchies, scoped delegation, and the confused deputy — measured.

1The problem

When Agent A delegates to B which calls C to modify a production database, reconstructing who authorized what is technically difficult and legally uncertain. Agents hold credentials that can be redirected by instructions hidden in data they consume (the confused deputy); delegation converts scoped access into ambient privilege; and multi-principal workflows (buyer, merchant, payment service) can complete while violating one principal's instructions.

2The current gap

Observability tools log actions but not authority: no principal hierarchy, no per-hop scope attenuation, no provenance distinguishing instruction-channel from data-channel content. Governance literature calls attribution the hardest question of 2026 — this system makes it measurable.

3How the solution addresses it

A principal hierarchy (user > developer > deployer > third-party) with provenance-tagged instructions; scoped, time-bounded authority tokens with per-hop attenuation and strict/lax delegation modes; a confused-deputy simulator with 4 defense policies; a multi-principal purchase workflow with semantic conflict detection and precedence resolution; and an intent checker validating actions against stated constraints.

4Architecture

Blueprint of the Delegation Integrity architecture. See the repo for the full implementation.

6Insights

  • Every hop is another injection chance. Attack success climbs 0.39 → 0.96 with depth — attenuation is not optional.
  • Without enforced attenuation, 3 in 4 delegations leak. Lax mode's 0.735 ambient-privilege rate is the default in most frameworks.
  • Attribution is structural. Without provenance tokens it decays as 1/d; with them it holds at 1.000.
